Logo only, or full branding? A decision framework

Designers have an obvious interest in telling you to buy the bigger package. So here is the opposite: the specific cases where a logo on its own is the correct purchase, the three situations where it will definitely cost you more later, and a checklist that answers it in two minutes.

Muhammed Ramees Updated October 2026 8 min read

The short answer

  • A logo is enough if you are pre-revenue and testing, operate on one channel, one person makes all the content, and you print nothing.
  • You need a full identity if you have a physical product, more than one person creating content, or you need Arabic.
  • Those three cases are where a logo-only purchase gets redone — typically within 6 to 18 months, at a total higher than buying the identity once.
  • A logo-only project in the UAE runs AED 500–6,000; a logo plus a working identity system runs AED 2,000–9,000. The overlap is the point.
  • The minimum viable identity is five things: logo system, two or three colours with print values, two fonts, one layout rule, and the source files.
  • If you can only buy one thing, buy correct files and clear ownership over a nicer-looking mark.

This is the most common question I get in a first conversation, usually phrased as "do I really need all the brand guidelines, or can I just get a logo?"

The honest answer is that it depends on four things, and none of them is your budget. It depends on how many people will use the brand, whether anything gets printed, how many languages you operate in, and how certain you are that the business will still look like this in a year.

I have told founders to buy a logo only. I have also watched founders spend AED 1,200 on a logo and then AED 3,500 eight months later rebuilding what the logo should have come with. Both outcomes are predictable from the four questions.

First, what each thing actually is

The terms get used loosely, so let me pin them down. These are not marketing tiers, they are different deliverables.

What you buyYou receiveUAE price
Logo One mark, usually one arrangement. Vector file if you are lucky. No colour spec beyond a HEX, no type system, no usage rules. AED 500–3,000
Logo system The mark plus horizontal, stacked, symbol-only, one-colour and reversed versions, with clear space and minimum sizes. AED 1,500–6,000
Brand identity The logo system plus colour in HEX/RGB/CMYK/Pantone, a type scale, imagery direction, graphic devices, and a brand book. AED 2,000–9,000
Brand identity + applications All of the above, applied: packaging, stationery, signage artwork, social templates, a company profile or a website. AED 4,000–20,000

Notice the overlap between rows two and three. In this market, a good logo system and a basic brand identity are often within AED 1,000 of each other, because the extra work is documentation rather than design. That overlap is why "logo only" is often a false saving — you are declining a document, not declining design hours.

When a logo genuinely is enough

Four situations where I would tell you to buy the logo and stop. Not grudgingly — this is the right call.

1. You are pre-revenue and testing the idea

If you have not sold anything yet, you are buying a brand for a business whose shape you do not know. The positioning will move. The audience may move. The name might move.

Buy a clean logo and a colour. Launch. Sell something. A full identity built on pre-launch assumptions gets rebuilt the moment real customers tell you who you actually are, and then you have paid twice for the same document.

The one condition: get the vector source file and written ownership even at this stage. A cheap logo you own can be extended. A cheap logo you only have as a PNG has to be redrawn.

2. You operate on exactly one channel

A business that exists entirely on Instagram, or entirely on a single marketplace listing, or entirely through WhatsApp orders, has one visual context to get right. Brand consistency is a problem created by having many surfaces. With one surface, you solve it by being careful.

This holds for home bakers, single-service freelancers, a tutor, a personal trainer, a photographer. Add a second channel and the calculation changes fast.

3. One person makes every piece of content

A brand book is coordination infrastructure. Its entire purpose is to let people who are not you produce work that looks like yours. If you are the only person who will ever touch a design file, the rules are in your head and the document is overhead.

The moment you hire a social media freelancer, bring on a cofounder with opinions, or hand files to a printer, you stop being that business.

4. It is a side project, or has a known end date

A pop-up for one Ramadan season, a single-event brand, a side project you will probably abandon, an internal tool. Match the investment to the lifespan. A logo and a colour is fine. Spend the saved money on reach.

The rule that decides it

Buy a logo if you are the only person who will ever apply it, and nothing will be printed. Buy a full identity the moment either of those becomes false — because at that point the cost of inconsistency starts compounding faster than the cost of the document.

The three situations where stopping at a logo costs more later

These are not hypothetical. These are the three reasons founders come back to us with a logo someone else made and a problem it cannot solve.

Situation one: you have a physical product or packaging

Packaging is where an undocumented logo becomes expensive, and it happens in a specific sequence.

You send the printer a logo. They ask for CMYK or Pantone values. You do not have them, so they convert from your HEX, and the printed colour comes back noticeably different from your Instagram. You accept it, because 2,000 units are already run. Now your online and offline brand are two different colours.

Then the next problem: the logo is 38 mm wide on a box front and 14 mm on a bottle cap, and at 14 mm the detail fills in and the tagline is unreadable. Nobody drew a small-size version, so the printer scales the big one and it looks muddy.

Then the third: you want a second SKU. The first box's layout was invented by the printer's in-house designer. There is no system to follow, so SKU two looks like a different brand on the same shelf.

For Heidi Hair and Glow the packaging was in scope from the start, which meant the mark was tested at cap size before it was approved, the palette was specified in Pantone before anything went to print, and the label system was built to take new products without redesign. That sequencing is the whole saving.

Cost of fixing it after the fact: AED 1,500–4,000 to rebuild the system, plus the print run you already paid for. In the UAE, reprinting 2,000 labels with corrected colour typically runs AED 800–2,500 on its own.

Situation two: more than one person creates content

The second a freelancer, an agency, a cousin who is good at Canva, or a new hire starts making posts, your brand becomes an average of everyone's taste.

What that looks like in practice: three shades of your brand colour across nine posts, because each person eyedropped it from a different JPEG. Four fonts. The logo stretched on one post and shadowed on another. Captions in a different voice every week. Nobody did anything wrong — there were no rules to follow.

The real cost is not ugliness, it is recognition. A customer needs to see a brand several times before they remember it, and inconsistent assets reset that count. You are paying for reach and then discarding the compounding.

Cost of fixing it after the fact: AED 2,000–5,000 for a retro-fit identity and templates, plus the time spent redoing published material you care about.

Situation three: your business needs Arabic

This is the most expensive one to postpone, and the most specific to this region.

An Arabic lockup is not a translation step, it is a drawing step. Arabic and Latin have different baselines, different x-heights, different stroke rhythms and different natural proportions. If the Latin wordmark was designed without the Arabic ever being considered, the two will not match — and the fix is usually to redraw the Latin, not just add the Arabic.

I have had this conversation several times: a founder arrives with a Latin logo they like, asks for "the Arabic version", and the correct answer is that the Latin mark has features — a very high x-height, a tight tracking, a geometric construction — that no honest Arabic treatment can mirror. Adding Arabic means reopening the Latin.

For Salkaara, an Arabic cuisine restaurant, we went the other direction deliberately. The Latin wordmark took its sweeping terminals from Arabic calligraphy from the first sketch, and a second lockup rendered the name in an Arabic-styled form. Because both scripts were in the room at the start, the two lockups are genuinely siblings rather than one being a retrofit of the other. That is only possible if bilingual is a brief, not an afterthought.

Cost of fixing it after the fact: AED 1,500–6,000 to add a properly matched Arabic lockup, and significantly more if the Latin has to be reworked. Building bilingual from the start typically adds 30–50% to a single-script identity — which is less than doing it twice.

The decision checklist

Answer these ten. Count your yeses in each column.

How to read your score:

  • Three or more "full identity" yeses — buy the identity. The logo-only route will be redone.
  • One or two "full identity" yeses — buy a logo system with print-ready colour values and a short guide. Middle option, genuinely sufficient.
  • Zero "full identity" yeses — buy a logo, own the files, spend the rest on getting customers.
  • Any Arabic yes — treat it as decisive on its own. It is the one answer that changes how the Latin mark is drawn.
Nobody regrets owning a vector file. Plenty of founders regret the PNG they could not scale.

The middle option nobody offers you

Most studios present two choices: a cheap logo or a full programme. There is a genuinely useful middle, and if you are a founder who answered one or two yeses, this is what to ask for.

Call it the minimum viable identity. Five things:

  1. A logo system — primary, horizontal, stacked, one-colour and reversed. Five files, not one.
  2. Two or three colours, each specified in HEX, RGB, CMYK and Pantone. Four values each, so a printer never guesses.
  3. Two typefaces — one display, one body — with licensing in your name or open-licence faces, plus a system fallback.
  4. One layout rule — where the logo sits, how much margin it gets, minimum sizes. A single page.
  5. Every source file, with ownership assigned in writing.

That is a four to six page document and roughly 60% of the useful content of a 30-page brand book, at a fraction of the documentation time. Our Launch package at AED 1,999 is built to exactly this shape, which is why it exists as a separate tier rather than a discount on the AED 2,999 Identity package. Two revision rounds, 50/50 payment, source files on both.

What to insist on even in the cheapest possible version

If your budget is AED 800 and nothing I have written changes that, these four lines are non-negotiable. They cost the designer almost nothing and they are the difference between a logo you can build on and a logo you will throw away.

  • The vector source file — .AI or .SVG, fonts converted to outlines. Not just PNGs.
  • Written ownership transfer on final payment. Marketplace sellers frequently licence rather than assign; you cannot trademark something you only licence.
  • CMYK and Pantone values for your colours, even if you have no print plans. Ten minutes of work, saves a mismatch.
  • Font licence confirmation — either a licence in your name or a commercially free face named explicitly.

I would rather a founder buy a plainer logo with these four things than a prettier one without them. The first can grow into a brand. The second is a dead end you pay for twice.

Frequently asked

Can I start with a logo and add the full identity later?

Yes, and it works well — on two conditions. You must own the vector source file, and the logo must already have been drawn as a system with variations, not as a single arrangement. If both are true, extending it later costs roughly the same as having bought the identity upfront. If either is false, extending means redrawing, and you pay for the logo twice.

Is a logo enough for a UAE trade licence?

A trade licence does not require a logo at all. But the practical first needs of a newly licensed business — a bank account application, a Google Business Profile, a signboard, an Ejari or tenancy fit-out, printed cards for networking — all want artwork, and at least one of them will want a print-ready vector file. Having vector files and print colour values ready at licensing stage is worth more than having a brand book.

Do I need brand guidelines if I use Canva for everything?

More than if you did not. Canva makes it easy for several people to produce divergent work quickly. The useful answer is a short guide plus a set of correct Canva templates with the palette and fonts already locked in — so the easy path is also the on-brand path.

What if my budget only covers a logo but I have packaging coming?

Spend the logo budget, but spend it on the right logo: one tested at small size, with Pantone values specified and one-colour versions drawn. Tell the designer packaging is coming. Those three requests cost very little extra and they prevent the three expensive packaging failures. The brand book can wait; the production-readiness cannot.
