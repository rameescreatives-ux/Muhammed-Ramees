Hiring

Agency, freelancer or small studio: what a UAE startup actually gets for its branding budget

I run a small studio, so you should read this knowing that. I have also tried to write it so that it would survive being read by an agency creative director and a good freelancer — because the cases where they are the better hire are real, and specific.

Muhammed Ramees Updated October 2026 9 min read

The short answer

  • Freelancer — AED 500–6,000. Best for a tiny budget, a single asset, or when you can art-direct yourself. Highest variance in quality, lowest continuity.
  • Small studio — AED 2,000–20,000. Best for first full identities, bilingual work and founder-led businesses that want one accountable person. Limited capacity, can't absorb a multi-market programme.
  • Agency — AED 15,000–250,000+. Genuinely the right call for multi-market rollouts, regulated industries, many stakeholders, procurement processes and large budgets.
  • In an agency fee, roughly 45–65% is not design work — it is account management, new business, offices and overhead. That is not waste; it is what buys process and capacity.
  • Ask who is actually doing the work. The single most predictive question across all three options.
  • The real risk ranking is: freelancer = disappearance risk, studio = capacity risk, agency = dilution risk.

In the UAE you have three real options for getting a brand designed, and the marketing from all three is roughly identical. Everyone says they are strategic, everyone shows a case study, everyone promises to understand your business.

The actual differences are structural: how many people are between you and the person drawing, what happens when something goes wrong, and how much of your fee pays for something other than design. Those three things determine almost everything else.

Where the money actually goes

This is the part founders find most useful and the part least often explained. A branding fee is not mostly design hours in any of the three models — but the proportion varies enormously.

Cost componentFreelancerSmall studioAgency
Design and craft time~85%~70%~35–55%
Account & project management0%~5%~15–20%
New business & pitching~5%~10%~15–25%
Premises & overhead~5%~10%~10–20%
Strategy & research~5%~5%~10–15%

These are indicative, not audited, but the shape is right and it explains the price gap better than any claim about quality. An agency quoting AED 60,000 for an identity is not necessarily charging twenty times a studio's margin. It is carrying an account team, a Dubai office, a pitch function and a finance department inside the number.

The thing nobody says out loud

Overhead is not fraud. What you buy with an agency's overhead is capacity, process and institutional survival — the ability to run nine workstreams at once, to absorb a designer leaving mid-project, and to still exist in three years when you need the master files.

The honest question is not "is the overhead justified?" It is "do I need what the overhead buys?" A single-market startup with one decision-maker usually does not. A regional retailer launching in four countries absolutely does.

When an agency is genuinely the right call

I will lose work by writing this clearly, but these are real and a small studio telling you otherwise is selling.

  1. Multi-market or multi-country rollout. If the brand launches in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Kuwait, you need parallel capacity, local cultural and linguistic review in each market, and someone coordinating it. One or two people cannot do that on a shared timeline.
  2. Regulated industries. Healthcare, financial services, insurance, pharmaceuticals, education, government-adjacent work. Compliance review loops are slow, specific and unforgiving, and agencies have done them before. A studio learning your regulator's requirements on your project is a risk you are paying for.
  3. Many stakeholders. If eight people have to approve, the project is a stakeholder-management exercise with a design component. Agencies have a whole discipline for this — workshops, structured presentations, documented rationale, consensus-building. Small studios are usually bad at it and should admit so.
  4. Naming with legal clearance. Generating names and clearing them for trademark across multiple classes and jurisdictions is specialist work with real legal cost attached. Expect AED 20,000–80,000 and use someone who does it routinely.
  5. Real primary research. Quantitative customer research, category audits, brand tracking studies. This needs fieldwork and specialists, not designers.
  6. Procurement requirements. If your organisation requires three pre-qualified bidders, professional indemnity insurance, audited accounts and a formal RFP response, a two-person studio cannot comply. That is a legitimate reason, not a quality judgement.
  7. Budget above roughly AED 60,000 with a long horizon. Above that level you are buying a programme, not a project, and programme management is what agencies are structurally built for.

When a freelancer is genuinely the right call

Equally real. The best freelancers in this market are excellent and significantly cheaper than any studio, and there are four situations where hiring one is simply correct.

  1. Your total budget is under AED 2,000. At that level you cannot buy a studio's full process, and a good freelancer will give you more design for the money than a cheap studio package will.
  2. You need one specific asset. A pitch deck, a menu, an Instagram template set, a single packaging dieline, an illustration. Discrete, specified, one-off work is what freelancers are most efficient at.
  3. You can art-direct yourself. If you have design judgement — a founder with a design background, or a marketing lead who has run brand projects — you are supplying the direction a studio would otherwise charge you for. Hiring hands rather than a brain is rational and cheaper.
  4. You need extra capacity, not a new perspective. Resizing an existing identity across forty applications, production artwork, adapting approved layouts. Execution work against a defined system.

What freelance work needs in exchange is diligence from you. Four things to insist on, in writing, before any payment:

  • Source files — editable vector, fonts outlined. Named as a deliverable, not implied.
  • Written ownership transfer on final payment. Marketplace sellers frequently licence rather than assign, which blocks trademark registration.
  • Font licences — in your name, or an explicitly commercially-free typeface.
  • Staged payment, with the final tranche released on receipt of files, not on approval of a JPEG.

The failure mode I see most is not a bad freelance logo. It is a perfectly good freelance logo that arrived as a PNG from a person who has since stopped replying.

Where the small studio sits

A two-to-six person studio is the middle option, and its advantages are entirely structural rather than talent-based. Good designers exist in all three models.

What the structure actually gives you:

  • No translation layer. You brief the person who draws. Nothing is paraphrased by an account manager, which removes the most common source of rounds spent fixing a misunderstanding.
  • Fewer rounds for a different reason. There is no internal review before you see work, so you see thinking earlier and redirect it earlier.
  • Lower cost at comparable craft, because the overhead listed in the table above is small.
  • Real continuity — the same person who drew the logo is there in eighteen months when you need a new SKU, and they remember why the decisions were made.
  • Enough breadth for a sequence — identity, then packaging, then a website — which a single freelancer usually cannot cover well.

And the honest limitations:

  • Capacity is the binding constraint. Two simultaneous launches is a real problem. Ask directly what else is in the studio's pipeline during your dates.
  • No formal research function. We can position a business sensibly; we cannot run a quantitative study, and you should not pay us to pretend otherwise.
  • Weak at committee management. If your project needs eight approvers aligned through facilitated workshops, that is not what a small studio is built for.
  • Business continuity risk. Small studios close. The mitigation is simple and you should demand it: full source files in your possession from the start, ownership assigned in writing.

For context on our own pricing: Launch at AED 1,999, Identity at AED 2,999, Complete at AED 4,999, plus Custom for anything outside those. Two revision rounds, 50/50 payment, source files always handed over. Those numbers sit below most UAE studios, and the reason is the overhead table above rather than a discount.

The best predictor of a good branding project is not the size of the firm. It is whether the person who understands your business is also the person moving the cursor.

The nine questions that work on all three

Ask these identically of a freelancer, a studio and an agency. The answers separate them more honestly than any portfolio.

  1. Who specifically will design this, and will I speak to them directly during the project?
  2. Show me the work of that specific person, not the firm's showreel.
  3. How many initial directions, and how many revision rounds are included before extra fees start?
  4. What exactly is in the guidelines — how many pages, which sections?
  5. Which source files do I receive, and is full ownership assigned to me in writing?
  6. Who holds the font licences, and who pays for them?
  7. Is an Arabic lockup included or quoted separately? (In this market, the answer tells you how well they know the region.)
  8. What does a small change cost in six months, and what is the turnaround?
  9. What else are you working on during my timeline?

Question one is the most predictive. At an agency, the honest answer is often "a mid-weight designer, overseen by our creative director" — which may be completely fine, but you should know it before you pay senior-level rates. At a freelancer and a small studio the answer should be unambiguous, and if it is not, something is being subcontracted that you have not been told about.

Question nine is the one nobody asks and everybody should. Capacity, not talent, is what causes most missed deadlines in this industry.

Frequently asked

Is a freelancer always cheaper than a studio?

Not reliably. A freelancer's day rate is lower, but a studio package often bundles work a freelancer quotes separately — logo variations, a brand book, Arabic lockups, templates, print-ready colour specification. In the UAE, a complete freelance identity with all of those billed individually frequently lands between AED 3,000 and 7,000, which overlaps directly with small-studio packages. Compare total deliverables, not hourly rates.

Will an agency produce better design than a small studio?

Not predictably, at the level of the artwork itself. Agencies produce better process: research, stakeholder alignment, rollout management, multi-market coordination. The individual craft depends on the individual designer, and the designer on your agency project may be more junior than the principal of a studio. If what you need is a well-made identity for a single market, the structural advantages of an agency are not the thing you are short of.

What happens to my brand if my studio or freelancer closes?

Nothing, if you hold the source files and written ownership. Everything, if you do not. This is the entire mitigation for the continuity risk of hiring small, and it is why we hand over editable source files on every project as standard. Ask for them at quoting stage; a refusal at that point is the answer to the question.

Can I use an agency for strategy and a studio for execution?

Yes, and for mid-sized businesses it can be the most efficient split — buy the research and positioning where it is genuinely deeper, then buy the design and rollout where it is cheaper. Two conditions make it work: the strategy must arrive as a written, decision-ready document rather than a deck of adjectives, and the studio must be briefed before the strategy is finalised so it can flag what is not designable.
