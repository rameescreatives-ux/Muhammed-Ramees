What's actually inside a brand book?

"Brand guidelines included" is the vaguest line in any design quote. Here is the full section-by-section contents of a real brand book, what each page is for, how to recognise a thin one, and the file formats you should refuse to leave without.

Muhammed Ramees Updated October 2026 9 min read

The short answer

  • A proper brand book has eight core sections: brand basics, logo system, clear space and sizing, misuse, colour, typography, imagery and graphic devices, and applications.
  • 20–35 pages is the useful range for a small or mid-sized business. Under 8 pages is a cover sheet; over 60 is usually unread.
  • Colour must be specified in HEX, RGB, CMYK and Pantone — four values per colour, not one.
  • Insist on vector source files (AI, EPS, SVG) plus a flattened PDF of the book and the editable INDD or Figma file.
  • A thin brand book shows the logo on a white page and stops. A real one shows the logo failing, and tells you why.
  • Budget AED 2,000–9,000 for a studio-made brand book in the UAE; our Identity package at AED 2,999 includes a 20+ page book.

I have opened a lot of brand books handed over by other studios, usually because a client has come to us to extend or fix an identity. The pattern is consistent. The expensive ones are often bloated with mood photography and strategy language nobody will read. The cheap ones are four slides: logo, colours, two fonts, thank you.

Both fail the same test. A brand book exists so that somebody who has never met the designer can produce something on-brand without asking a question. If a printer, a signage company, a freelance social media manager and a web developer can all do their jobs from the document, it works. If any of them have to email you asking "what's the CMYK?", it does not.

The eight sections every brand book should contain

This is the structure we build to, and it is close to standard across serious studios. Page counts are what we typically allocate in a 24–30 page book.

SectionPagesWhat it must contain
01 Brand basics 2–3 What the brand is, who it serves, the positioning in one paragraph, and three to five personality attributes. Tone-of-voice notes if verbal identity is in scope.
02 Logo system 4–6 Primary lockup, secondary lockup, stacked and horizontal versions, the icon or symbol alone, the wordmark alone, one-colour and reversed versions.
03 Clear space & sizing 2 Exclusion zone defined by a unit from the logo itself, minimum print size in mm, minimum digital size in px.
04 Misuse 1–2 Six to ten crossed-out examples of the logo done wrong, each with a one-line reason.
05 Colour 3–4 Primary, secondary and neutral palettes with HEX, RGB, CMYK and Pantone for each. Proportion guidance and accessible text-on-colour pairings.
06 Typography 3–4 Display and body typefaces, the weights licensed, a type scale with sizes and line heights, and web fallbacks.
07 Imagery & devices 3–5 Photography direction with do/don't examples, illustration or icon style, patterns, shapes, and any supporting graphic element.
08 Applications 4–8 The identity shown on real things: stationery, signage, social templates, packaging, vehicle, uniform, email signature — whatever your business actually uses.

Section 01 — Brand basics

Short. Two pages. The job here is to tell a new freelancer, in ninety seconds, what this brand is trying to be so their design instincts point the right way.

What belongs: a one-paragraph positioning statement, the audience in plain terms, and three to five adjectives with an anti-adjective each. "Warm, not folksy. Precise, not clinical." The anti-adjective is the part that actually controls decisions.

What does not belong: a twelve-page strategy deck, a mission statement written by committee, or stock photography of people in meetings. If your brand book opens with four pages before the logo appears, somebody is padding.

Section 02 — The logo system

This is the section most thin brand books fake. One logo on one white page is not a system. A system means every shape of space your brand will ever have to fit into has a version waiting for it.

What should be drawn, named and supplied as a separate file:

  • Primary lockup — the full signature, symbol plus wordmark, in its default arrangement.
  • Horizontal lockup — for website headers, email signatures, and wide signage.
  • Stacked lockup — for square and portrait spaces: social avatars, packaging panels, tote bags.
  • Symbol / icon alone — for favicons, app icons, stamps, and embroidery.
  • Wordmark alone — for cases where the symbol is already present nearby.
  • One-colour version — solid black and solid white, for engraving, embossing, fax-grade printing and single-colour merchandise.
  • Reversed version — tested on dark backgrounds, with any adjustments the mark needs to hold up there.
  • Arabic lockup, if you operate in the GCC — and ideally a bilingual lockup where both scripts sit together correctly.

Each of those should appear in the book, labelled, with a note on when to use it. "Use the stacked lockup where the available width is less than 2.5× the logo height" is a usable rule. "Use as appropriate" is not.

Section 03 — Clear space and minimum sizes

Two pages that save you from a thousand bad applications. Clear space is the protected margin around the logo where nothing else is allowed — not text, not another logo, not the edge of the page.

The correct way to define it is relative to the logo itself, so it scales. Take a measurement from inside the mark — the height of the letter "x" in the wordmark, the width of the symbol's stem, the diameter of a counter — call it X, and specify the exclusion zone as 1X or 0.5X on all four sides. Never define clear space in millimetres, because it stops being correct the moment the logo is resized.

Minimum sizes need two numbers, because print and screen fail differently:

  • Minimum print width — usually 20–25 mm for a lockup with a tagline, 12–15 mm for a wordmark, 8 mm for a symbol.
  • Minimum digital width — usually 90–120 px for a lockup, 32 px for a symbol, 16 px for a favicon-grade variant.

If the primary lockup breaks below 25 mm, the book should say so and point you to the simplified version instead. That instruction is worth more than any mood board in the document.

Section 04 — Misuse

My favourite page to make, and the fastest way to judge a brand book's quality. A good misuse page shows the logo actually broken, with a red cross and a one-line reason. It is the only section that anticipates what will go wrong.

The ten misuses worth showing:

  1. Stretched or squashed — non-proportional scaling.
  2. Rotated at an angle.
  3. Recoloured outside the palette.
  4. Drop shadow, bevel, outer glow or gradient added.
  5. Outlined or given a stroke.
  6. Placed on a busy photograph with no contrast.
  7. Placed on a palette colour it was never cleared against.
  8. Lockup rearranged — symbol moved, spacing altered, tagline resized.
  9. Typeface substituted in the wordmark.
  10. Clear space violated by adjacent text or a page edge.

The one-page test

If you only look at a single page to judge a brand book, look at the misuse page. A studio that has drawn ten specific ways their own logo fails has stress-tested the mark. A studio that skipped it has shown you the logo only in the one situation where it looks good.

Section 05 — Colour, specified four ways

This is where thin brand books cost you money directly. A colour given only as a HEX code cannot be printed accurately, and a printer who has to guess will give you a logo that does not match your website.

Every colour in the palette needs four values:

ValueUsed forWhy you need it
HEXWeb, apps, emailThe format every developer and CMS expects.
RGBScreens, video, presentationsWhat Office, Canva and video editors ask for.
CMYKOffset and digital printBrochures, business cards, packaging. A HEX converted badly to CMYK goes muddy.
Pantone (PMS)Spot printing, signage, textiles, vinylThe only way to guarantee the same colour across a box, a sign and a uniform.

If your brand uses signage, packaging or merchandise, also ask for the RAL or vinyl reference and whether the Pantone is coated (C) or uncoated (U). These are small lines in a document that prevent a signage company in Mussafah phoning you with a question you cannot answer.

The colour section should also include proportion — roughly how much of each colour to use, often shown as a simple bar — and accessible pairings: which text colours are legible on which background, ideally with the contrast ratio noted. For a brand that will live mostly on a website, that last page is not optional.

Section 06 — Typography and the type scale

Naming two fonts is not a typography section. What makes it usable is the scale: a table of every text level, with size, weight, line height and letter spacing.

A workable type scale looks like this:

LevelSize / line heightWeight & tracking
Display / H148–64 px / 1.05Display face, 600, −3% tracking
H230–36 px / 1.15Display face, 600, −2%
H320–23 px / 1.3Display face, 500, −1%
Body16–17 px / 1.7Body face, 400, 0
Small / caption12–13 px / 1.5Body face, 500, +8%
Overline / label11 px / 1.2Body face, 600, +14%, uppercase

Alongside the scale, the section must state:

  • Which weights are licensed, and for what — desktop, web, app, e-pub. A font licensed for desktop only cannot legally be loaded on your website.
  • Who holds the licence — you or the studio. If it is the studio's, you have a problem the day you hire someone else.
  • System fallbacks — what to use in PowerPoint, Google Docs, email signatures and anywhere the brand font is unavailable. Usually Arial, Georgia or a specified Google font.
  • The Arabic typeface, if relevant, with its own weights and the vertical adjustments needed to sit alongside the Latin.
A brand book is not a portfolio of your identity. It is an instruction manual for people who will never speak to the designer.

Section 07 — Imagery and graphic devices

This is the section that stops a brand drifting. Without it, every new freelancer imports their own taste in photography and the Instagram grid turns into four different brands in six months.

What it should contain:

  • Photography direction — six to nine example images that are on-brand, and three that are off-brand with a reason. Note the treatment: natural light or studio, warm or neutral grade, tight crops or wide, people or product.
  • Illustration or icon style — stroke weight, corner radius, whether icons are filled or outlined, and the grid they are drawn on.
  • Graphic devices — the supporting shapes that carry the brand when the logo is absent: a pattern, a frame, a corner cut, a rule weight, a shape derived from the mark. This is what lets a social post be recognisably yours without putting the logo on it.
  • Layout principles — grid, margins, and how much empty space the brand wants. One page is enough.

Section 08 — Applications

The closing section, and the one clients actually enjoy. The identity shown on real objects. Its purpose is partly to demonstrate the system works, and partly to give suppliers a visual reference they can copy.

Which applications appear should depend entirely on your business. A restaurant needs menus, signage, packaging, uniform and delivery stickers. A distributor needs a company profile, invoices, vehicle livery, carton marking and an email template. A clinic needs reception signage, appointment cards and WhatsApp templates. A book that shows a tote bag and a coffee cup for a B2B logistics company is decoration, not documentation.

When we built the identity for Heidi Hair and Glow, the applications section carried more weight than the rest of the book, because a beauty brand lives on physical packaging — label dielines, bottle wraps, box panels and the small-size behaviour of the mark at 15 mm on a cap. For FreshRoutes International, the same section was mostly document templates and carton marking, because that is where a produce distributor's brand is actually seen.

The template-book giveaway is worth dwelling on. If the applications are a billboard, a skyscraper sign and a branded aeroplane, and your business is a twelve-seat café, the studio has filled a template. That is not automatically fatal — the logo may still be good — but you are not holding a document built for you.

The file formats to insist on

This is the part founders most often get wrong, because the deliverables list in a quote says "all files" and nobody checks what arrived. Here is what "all files" should mean.

AssetFormats you needWhy
Logo — master .AI or .SVG (vector, editable, fonts outlined) Infinitely scalable. The one file from which everything else is made. Fonts must be converted to outlines so nobody needs your typeface.
Logo — print .EPS and .PDF, CMYK and Pantone versions What every printer and signage firm in the UAE will ask for by name.
Logo — screen .SVG plus .PNG at 500 / 1000 / 2000 px, transparent SVG for the website, PNGs for everyone who cannot open an SVG.
Logo — favicon & app .ICO, plus 512 and 1024 px square PNG Browser tab, WhatsApp Business, app stores, Google Business Profile.
Brand book Print-ready .PDF and the editable .INDD or Figma file The editable file is what lets you or any future designer add a section.
Templates Native (.INDD, .AI, .PSD, Figma) plus an editable Canva link where useful Your team will edit social posts in Canva whether the brand book approves or not. Give them a correct Canva file.
Fonts Licence certificate or purchase receipt, plus the files Proof you may legally use them, in your name.
Colour .ASE swatch library Loads the exact palette into Adobe apps for any future designer.

Two contractual lines matter as much as the files. First, that full ownership is assigned to you in writing on final payment — not licensed. Second, that fonts are licensed for commercial use in your name, or are open-licence faces that need no purchase. We hand over source files on every project as a matter of course, including the editable brand book, because a brand you cannot edit is a brand you are renting.

Frequently asked

What is the difference between brand guidelines and a brand book?

Nothing meaningful — the terms are used interchangeably, along with "brand manual" and "visual identity guidelines". What matters is the contents, not the name. Some studios distinguish a short "guidelines" document from a longer "brand book" that also covers strategy and verbal identity, but there is no industry standard, so always ask for the page count and the section list.

How many pages should a brand book be?

For most small and mid-sized businesses, 20–35 pages. Under 8 pages cannot cover logo variations, four-value colour, a type scale and misuse. Over 60 pages is usually only justified for multi-market, multi-brand or franchise organisations, and in practice tends to go unread.

Do I really need Pantone colours if I only sell online?

You need them the first time you print anything — business cards, a pull-up banner at an exhibition, a shop sign, packaging, a uniform. Having them specified costs the designer ten minutes at the end of the project and costs you a colour mismatch if they skip it. Get them specified now even if you never use them.

Can I update a brand book myself later?

Only if you received the editable file. A flattened PDF cannot be meaningfully edited, which is exactly why some studios hand over nothing else. Ask for the InDesign or Figma source at the point of quoting, not at handover.
