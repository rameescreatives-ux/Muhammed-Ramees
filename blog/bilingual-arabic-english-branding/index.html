Bilingual

Bilingual branding in the GCC: building an Arabic + English identity that actually matches

Most "bilingual" logos in this region are a Latin wordmark with an Arabic font typed underneath it. That is not a bilingual identity — it is two unrelated pieces of lettering sharing a page. Here is what matching two scripts properly actually involves.

Muhammed Ramees Updated October 2026 11 min read

The short answer

  • A bilingual identity means two pieces of lettering drawn to match in weight, rhythm and optical size — not an Arabic font set under a Latin wordmark.
  • Arabic and Latin do not share a baseline. Arabic sits on a baseline with substantial letterforms descending below it, and matching the two means optical alignment, not numerical alignment.
  • Match stroke weight optically, not numerically. Arabic's connected strokes and higher density make a technically equal weight look 10–20% heavier.
  • Choose your Arabic style for meaning: Naskh for heritage, authority and readability; Kufi for geometric, modern, architectural; contemporary Arabic sans for tech and retail.
  • Build two lockups: horizontal (Arabic right, Latin left) and stacked (Arabic above or below). Not one squeezed into both.
  • Decide transliteration or translation at brief stage — it changes the entire drawing.
  • Budget 30–50% on top of a single-script identity. In the UAE that means roughly AED 1,500–6,000 extra at studio level.

This is the part of brand design in the GCC that gets done badly most often, and it is done badly for an understandable reason: the person making the decision usually reads one of the two scripts fluently and the other not at all. A founder who reads English but not Arabic cannot see that the Arabic under their logo is set in a default system font at the wrong weight. An Arabic-first founder cannot always see that the Latin is tracked too tight. So both sign off.

I have spent eight years working in a market where nearly every serious brand needs both scripts, and the single most useful thing I can tell you is this: a bilingual identity is not a translation task. It is a drawing task with two outputs that have to behave like siblings. Everything below follows from that.

Why dropping an Arabic font under a Latin wordmark fails

The default approach is: take the approved Latin wordmark, open a type menu, find an Arabic font that looks roughly similar in spirit, type the name, scale it to about the same width, centre it underneath. Done in twenty minutes.

Here is what breaks, specifically.

The weights will not match

Arabic letterforms connect. In a word, most letters join to the next, producing a continuous horizontal stroke along the baseline plus a dense cluster of above-baseline marks and dots. Latin letters are discrete, with white space between every one.

The result is that Arabic at the same measured stroke thickness reads heavier — typically 10 to 20 percent heavier to the eye — because the ink is more continuous and the counters are smaller. If you pair a Latin Medium with an Arabic Medium, the Arabic will look bolder. Matching means choosing a lighter Arabic weight, or adjusting stroke contrast by hand.

The optical sizes will not match

Scaling the Arabic to the same width as the Latin is the most common error, because width is the easiest thing to measure. But Arabic is naturally more compact horizontally — the same name often sets 15 to 30 percent narrower than its Latin equivalent — and forcing it to equal width means enlarging it until it visually dominates.

The correct reference is not width. It is the optical height of the main body — the height of the connected baseline forms in Arabic against the x-height of the Latin — and even that has to be judged by eye, not calculated.

The rhythm will not match

Latin wordmarks are often customised: tracking adjusted, two letters ligatured, a terminal extended, a counter opened up. Those choices give a mark its character. A stock Arabic font has none of them. So the Latin reads as designed and the Arabic reads as typed, and even a viewer who reads neither script can feel the mismatch.

The test that exposes a fake bilingual logo

Cover the Latin and look only at the Arabic. Does it look like it was drawn, with considered terminals, consistent stroke modulation and intentional spacing — or does it look like it was typed? If the Arabic could be reproduced by anyone with the same font, it is not part of your identity. It is a caption.

The technical problem: two scripts, different anatomy

To match them you have to know what you are matching. These are the five structural differences that matter in practice.

AttributeLatinArabic
Baseline behaviour Letters sit on the baseline; only g, j, p, q, y descend. Letters sit on the baseline but many forms — ج ح خ ع غ م ن ي ق ر و س ش — descend substantially below it as a normal feature, not an exception.
Vertical measure x-height governs apparent size; cap height and ascender define the top. There is no x-height. Apparent size is governed by the height of the connected baseline body, with alifs and lams rising above and dots and marks extending the vertical envelope further.
Connection Letters are discrete. Spacing is between letters. Most letters join. Each letter has up to four contextual forms — initial, medial, final, isolated — and the joined stroke is part of the word's shape.
Density Even distribution of ink and counter space. Denser along the baseline, with dots, hamzas and diacritics adding texture above and below.
Direction Left to right. Visual emphasis naturally falls left. Right to left. Visual emphasis falls right. Numerals usually still run left to right inside RTL text.

What "the Arabic baseline sits differently" means in practice

This is worth being concrete about, because it is the single most common cause of a bilingual lockup looking subtly wrong.

If you align a Latin wordmark and an Arabic wordmark on their shared typographic baseline — the mathematically correct thing to do — the Arabic will appear to float high. The reason is that Arabic's descending bowls are a large part of its visual mass, so the perceived centre of the Arabic word sits lower than its baseline, while the perceived centre of a Latin word sits roughly at half its x-height above the baseline.

The practical rule: in a horizontal bilingual lockup, align the two scripts by optical centre, not baseline. In most cases this means nudging the Arabic up relative to its true baseline by somewhere between 3 and 8 percent of the cap height, judged by eye at the size the lockup will actually be used. And it means re-checking it at small size, because the correct offset changes with scale.

There is no formula. Anyone who tells you there is a numerical rule for bilingual vertical alignment has not drawn many of them.

Choosing an Arabic type style, and what each one signals

Arabic has a deep calligraphic tradition and the styles are not interchangeable decoration — they carry meaning the way a serif or a geometric sans does in Latin. For branding, three families matter.

StyleCharacterSuits
Naskh The standard reading hand. Rounded, flowing, high stroke contrast, generous descenders, strong calligraphic lineage. Highly legible at text sizes and the default expectation for body copy. Heritage brands, hospitality, food, government and institutional, publishing, luxury, anything claiming authority or tradition. Pairs naturally with Latin serifs and humanist sans.
Kufi Angular, geometric, low contrast, architectural. Historically the monumental and inscriptional hand. Modern "geometric Kufi" is clean and highly constructible. Architecture, real estate, construction, technology, logistics, fashion, museums and cultural institutions. Pairs naturally with geometric Latin sans.
Contemporary / modern Arabic sans Simplified Naskh structure with reduced contrast, shortened descenders, more uniform stroke, designed for UI and signage. Often available with matched Latin companions. Tech, apps, retail, fintech, startups, healthcare, anything screen-first or requiring long bilingual UI text.
Diwani & Thuluth Highly calligraphic, ornamental, dense, with elaborate ligatures. Beautiful, hard to read, inherently ceremonial. Occasional use — a crest, a seal, a premium packaging accent, Ramadan or ceremonial material. Rarely correct as a primary wordmark, and almost never as body text.

Two practical notes. First, a brand usually needs two Arabic decisions, not one: a display treatment for the wordmark, which may be custom-drawn, and a text typeface for everything else — menus, websites, documents. They do not have to be the same family, but they do have to agree.

Second, be wary of pairing a modern geometric Latin with a highly calligraphic Arabic, or vice versa. Mismatched historical registers are the second-most common bilingual failure after mismatched weight. A geometric Latin sans with a geometric Kufi is coherent. The same Latin with Diwani reads as two brands.

How we handled it on Salkaara

Salkaara is an Arabic cuisine restaurant, and the brief was genuinely bilingual from the first conversation — which is the only reason the result works.

Rather than designing a Latin wordmark and then finding an Arabic companion, we started from the calligraphic form. The Latin wordmark takes its sweeping terminals from Arabic calligraphy — the stroke entries and exits, the way a terminal lifts and tapers rather than cutting flat, the modulation across a curve. So the Latin already carries Arabic structural DNA before any Arabic is drawn.

A second lockup then renders the name in an Arabic-styled form. Because the Latin was built from the same gestural logic, the two lockups read as siblings: same stroke modulation, same terminal treatment, same sense of hand. Neither is a retrofit of the other.

The lesson generalises. If you want two scripts to match, the cheapest way to get there is to let the shared characteristics influence whichever script you draw first. Doing it the other way round — finishing one script, then trying to make the second agree — is where the 30 to 50 percent cost premium mostly goes.

You cannot make two scripts match at the end of a project. You decide they will match at the beginning, or they do not.

Horizontal versus stacked bilingual lockups

You need both. A single bilingual arrangement will fail in half the places your brand has to appear.

The horizontal lockup

Two wordmarks side by side, usually with a dividing rule or sufficient clear space between them. Convention in the GCC places the Arabic on the right and the Latin on the left, which reads correctly in both directions: the Arabic reader meets Arabic first, the Latin reader meets Latin first.

What has to be specified:

  • The divider. A thin vertical rule, a dot, or nothing. If nothing, the gap must be wider than any internal letter gap in either script, or the two words read as one.
  • The optical alignment offset, as discussed above — vertical, by eye, documented as a value in the brand book so nobody re-derives it.
  • Which script carries the symbol, if you have one. Usually the symbol sits outside the pair, not between them.
  • A minimum width. A horizontal bilingual lockup is wide. Below roughly 45–55 mm in print, one or both scripts become unreadable, and you must fall back to the stacked version or a single-script version.

The stacked lockup

Arabic above Latin, or Latin above Arabic, centred. More compact, better for square spaces, social avatars, packaging panels, stamps and vertical signage.

Which goes on top is a positioning decision, not an aesthetic one. Arabic on top signals an Arabic-first brand — appropriate for a local heritage business, a government-adjacent organisation, or a brand whose primary audience is Arabic-speaking. Latin on top signals an international or expatriate-facing brand. Pick deliberately and keep it consistent; alternating looks indecisive.

The hard part of a stacked lockup is width matching. Because Arabic typically sets narrower, a centred stack can look unbalanced. Options, in order of preference: extend the Arabic using legitimate kashida elongation; adjust the Arabic letterforms' proportions slightly in the custom drawing; or accept an asymmetry and make it intentional rather than apologetic. Never stretch either script horizontally to fit.

The single-script fallbacks

A bilingual brand needs standalone Arabic-only and Latin-only lockups as well. An Arabic newspaper advertisement, a GCC government portal, an Arabic Instagram account, an export carton for a non-Arabic market — in each of those, carrying both scripts is clutter. Specify when each is used.

So the minimum bilingual logo set is six files, not two:

  1. Horizontal bilingual lockup
  2. Stacked bilingual lockup
  3. Latin-only lockup
  4. Arabic-only lockup
  5. Symbol alone
  6. One-colour and reversed versions of each of the above

Transliteration or translation? Decide at brief stage

What the Arabic actually says is a strategic decision that changes the drawing, so it cannot be left to the end.

ApproachWhat it doesWhen it is right
Transliteration Renders the sound of the Latin name in Arabic letters. The brand is spoken the same way in both languages. Invented or abstract names, personal names, international brands, anything where verbal recall across languages matters. The default choice for most businesses.
Translation Renders the meaning in Arabic, producing a different-sounding name. Descriptive names where the meaning is the asset ("Fresh Routes"), and institutional or government naming where meaning carries authority. Risky for consumer brands — you end up with two names and half the word-of-mouth.
Hybrid Transliterate the brand name, translate the descriptor or category line. Very common and usually correct: "Salkaara — مطعم" style constructions, where the name is constant and the descriptor adapts.

Three things to check before you commit to a transliteration:

  • Does it read as intended? Arabic short vowels are usually unwritten, so a transliteration can be read several ways. Have native speakers from more than one dialect region read it cold.
  • Does it accidentally mean something? A sound-based transliteration can collide with a real Arabic word. Check across Gulf, Levantine and Egyptian usage, not just Modern Standard Arabic.
  • How many letters does it produce? A six-letter Latin name can become four or nine Arabic letters depending on the transliteration chosen. That changes the width, the rhythm and the whole lockup geometry. Choose the spelling that also draws well.

RTL implications for websites and packaging

A bilingual identity is not finished until it survives being mirrored. This is where most bilingual projects quietly fail — the logo is fine and the Arabic website is a left-to-right layout with Arabic text poured into it.

On the web

  • Mirror the layout, not just the text. Set dir="rtl" and lang="ar" on the document. Navigation, logo position, sidebars, breadcrumbs, progress indicators, carousels and the reading order of columns all flip.
  • Use logical CSS properties. margin-inline-start rather than margin-left, padding-inline-end rather than padding-right, inset-inline rather than left/right. Written this way, one stylesheet serves both directions.
  • Do not mirror everything. Logos, photographs, phone numbers, email addresses, Latin brand names, numerals, charts with a time axis and media playback controls generally stay as they are. Directional arrows and back buttons should flip. Decide case by case.
  • Raise the Arabic font size. Arabic text at the same point size reads smaller and denser than Latin. A practical starting point is +8% to +15% on size and +10% on line height for the Arabic version. Tune by eye.
  • Do not use bold or italic as emphasis in Arabic. There is no italic in Arabic; synthetic obliques are wrong. Emphasise with weight only if the typeface has a real heavier weight, or with colour and spacing.
  • Avoid uppercase logic. Arabic has no capital letters, so any design built on all-caps labels needs a different Arabic treatment — usually weight or a lighter colour.
  • Test the fallback. If your Arabic webfont fails to load, the browser substitutes a system Arabic font with different metrics and your layout shifts. Specify the fallback stack deliberately.

On packaging and print

  • Panel order reverses. On a box designed for an Arabic-first reader, the "front" panel and the direction in which panels are read change. Confirm with the printer which panel is which before artwork.
  • UAE labelling. Consumer products sold in the UAE generally require Arabic on the label alongside English for product name, ingredients, and related mandatory information. Build the Arabic into the dieline from the first layout rather than finding space for it afterwards — this is the single most common packaging revision we see.
  • Bilingual ingredient lists need twice the area at a legible size. Designers consistently under-allocate it, then shrink the Arabic below readability to make it fit.
  • Spine and vertical text. Arabic does not rotate the same way Latin does. Vertical stacking of Arabic letters is wrong; a rotated baseline is usually the correct solution, and the rotation direction convention differs from Latin.

The eight most common bilingual mistakes

  1. Arabic typed in a system font under a custom Latin wordmark. The most frequent, and visible to every Arabic reader.
  2. Matching numerical weight instead of optical weight. Arabic Medium under Latin Medium reads bolder.
  3. Matching widths rather than optical heights. Produces an oversized, dominant Arabic.
  4. Horizontally stretching or condensing either script to make a lockup fit. Destroys stroke weight relationships in both.
  5. Baseline-aligning a horizontal lockup instead of optically aligning it, so the Arabic floats.
  6. Mismatched historical register — geometric Latin with ornate Diwani, or a calligraphic Latin script with a hard geometric Kufi.
  7. Letter-spacing the Arabic. Tracking Arabic letters apart breaks the connections and is simply incorrect; it is not a stylistic choice. Use kashida elongation if you need width.
  8. Treating the Arabic as a deliverable rather than part of the system. No Arabic minimum size, no Arabic clear space, no Arabic in the brand book's applications — so nobody knows how to use it.

Frequently asked

Can I just use an Arabic font instead of custom Arabic lettering?

Sometimes, yes — if you choose a well-made Arabic typeface with a matched Latin companion and then do the matching work: select the weight optically rather than by name, adjust the optical size against the Latin, refine the spacing, and optically align the lockup. That is a legitimate and much cheaper route, typically AED 800–2,000 at studio level. What does not work is typing the name in a default font at the same point size and calling it a lockup. The giveaway is always spacing and weight.

Should Arabic or English come first in the logo?

In a horizontal lockup, GCC convention puts Arabic on the right and Latin on the left, so each reader meets their own script first. In a stacked lockup, whichever script sits on top signals your primary audience: Arabic on top for local, heritage and institutional brands; Latin on top for international and expatriate-facing ones. Choose once and keep it consistent across every application.

Is Arabic legally required on packaging in the UAE?

For consumer products sold in the UAE, Arabic is generally required on the label alongside English for mandatory information such as product name and ingredients, and requirements vary by product category and emirate. Treat Arabic label content as a design constraint from the first dieline rather than a compliance step at the end — and confirm the current requirements for your specific category with your registration consultant or the relevant authority before printing.

Why does bilingual branding cost 30–50% more?

Because it is close to two identity projects that must agree with each other. You are paying for a second wordmark drawn or heavily refined rather than typed, two lockup geometries plus single-script fallbacks, a second type scale with its own sizes and line heights, RTL layout rules, and a brand book that documents both. The alternative — adding Arabic later — regularly costs more, because matching after the fact often means reopening the Latin.
